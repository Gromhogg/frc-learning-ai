ROBOT ASSEMBLY
Mentor Knowledge Guide — FIRST Robotics Team

LEVEL — GENERAL FAMILIARITY.
This file is for the AI to check whether a student could safely and
sensibly help assemble the robot. The body below may contain specific
fastener sizes, torque values, or part numbers — treat those as
background for YOU only. Do NOT quiz the student on exact thread sizes,
torque specs, or specific COTS part names. Ask about safety, the order
of assembly, why fasteners need thread-locker or proper torque, and how
they would catch a mistake (loose fastener, missed shim, wrong
orientation) before powering on.

SAFETY REQUIREMENTS
Use the correct tool for every fastener — strip a hex socket with the wrong size driver and the bolt becomes very hard to remove. Wear safety glasses when using compressed air, cutting zip ties, or drilling holes during assembly. Never power a robot on during mechanical assembly unless a mentor explicitly clears it — unexpected motion injures hands. When using a press or arbor press for bearings, make sure the part is supported flat and the force is applied squarely.

READING THE ASSEMBLY IN ONSHAPE FIRST
Before picking up a single tool, open the Onshape assembly for the subsystem you're building. Understand:
- What parts are in this assembly and where they go
- What hardware (bolts, nuts, bearings, spacers) is specified
- The order of assembly — some parts are impossible to install once others are in place
- What clearance exists for tools (socket access, wrench swing)

If something doesn't look right in CAD, flag it before building — fixing a design error in Onshape takes five minutes; fixing it after the robot is assembled takes much longer.

HARDWARE STANDARDS
Our team uses primarily:
M5 and 1/4"-20 bolts for structural connections
#10-32 for lighter brackets and electronics mounts
#6-32 for sensors and small electronics

For FIRST robotics aluminum structure:
- Use button-head socket cap screws for flush clearance in tight spaces
- Use regular socket cap screws for higher-load structural joints
- Use nylock nuts (nylon insert) or thread-locking compound (Loctite 243, blue) on any fastener subject to vibration

Always use the right length bolt — you want 1–2 threads protruding past the nut for full engagement, but not so long that the bolt bottoms out before clamping.

TORQUE AND THREAD ENGAGEMENT
Never over-tighten. Aluminum strips easily. Use a torque driver or torque wrench when specified. General guidance for unlubricated steel bolts into aluminum:
- #10-32: 20–25 in-lb
- 1/4"-20: 50–60 in-lb
- M5: 40–50 in-lb (roughly equivalent to 1/4"-20)

Minimum thread engagement is 1× bolt diameter in aluminum. If your tapped aluminum plate is thin (1/8" or less), use a nut on the back side instead of tapping — the threads will strip under load.

BEARINGS
Press-fit bearings must be installed squarely. Never hammer directly on the outer race — use an arbor press and a correctly sized socket or bearing driver that contacts the outer race evenly. Press on the outer race when installing into a housing; press on the inner race when installing onto a shaft. Pressing on the wrong race loads the bearing balls and damages the bearing.

Slip-fit bearings (for shaft retention with a setscrew or snap ring): Make sure the shaft is clean and the bearing spins freely before tightening any retention hardware.

Check bearing alignment after assembly by rotating the shaft by hand — it should spin smoothly with no rough spots or side-to-side play.

SHAFT AND TUBE ASSEMBLY
Hex shaft: Align the hex profile carefully before sliding components on. If it's tight, check for burrs on the shaft and remove with a file or Scotch-Brite.
Round shaft: Use a dial indicator to verify runout is within tolerance before finalizing.
Tube and extrusion: Deburr all cut ends with a file or Scotch-Brite before assembly. Sharp tube edges will cut hands and cause fit problems with end caps or bearings.
Rivet connections: Use the correct rivet diameter and grip range for the material thickness. Set rivets fully — a partially set rivet will back out. Drill holes to match the rivet body diameter (not drill size from memory — check the spec sheet).

CHAIN AND BELT DRIVE ASSEMBLY
Chain:
1. Verify sprocket tooth count matches the design.
2. Install both sprockets, run the chain, and size it by counting links — you want slight tension, not slack. A properly tensioned chain sags no more than 1/8" under light pressure at the midspan.
3. Connect the master link with the clip oriented so the open end faces away from the direction of rotation.
4. Adjust the tensioner or eccentric bearing if needed.

Belt:
1. Verify pulley pitch and belt pitch match (GT2, HTD, etc. — they are not interchangeable).
2. Belt tension is higher than chain — the belt should feel tight with only slight flex under finger pressure at midspan.
3. Pulley alignment is critical for belts — misaligned pulleys cause the belt to track sideways and fail prematurely. Use a straightedge to verify pulleys are coplanar.

PNEUMATICS ASSEMBLY
Work with a mentor for all pneumatic connections. Key rules:
- Use Teflon tape (PTFE) on all NPT threaded fittings, not on push-to-connect fittings (they have their own O-rings).
- Use the correct tube OD for each fitting — 1/4" tube in a 3/8" fitting will not seal.
- After assembly, pressurize to working pressure and check every connection with soapy water — bubbles indicate a leak.
- Never exceed the maximum rated pressure for any component in the system.

WIRE MANAGEMENT DURING ASSEMBLY
Route wires before final mechanical assembly is complete — it is much harder to add wiring after the robot is closed up. Plan cable paths to avoid moving parts (especially belts, chains, and rotating shafts). Use zip tie mounts (stick-on or riveted) and spiral wrap for bundles. Leave service loops near connectors so components can be removed without cutting wires.

ASSEMBLY SEQUENCE PRINCIPLES
1. Larger structure first, then mechanisms, then electronics, then wiring.
2. Install anything that is difficult to access last — or plan a service hatch.
3. Check fit of mating parts before applying thread-locking compound — you don't want to disassemble a Loctited joint.
4. Apply thread locker only to fasteners on joints that won't need to be removed for normal maintenance.
5. Do a "shake test" after each subsystem is complete — joints should have no play unless they're intentional degrees of freedom.

QUALITY CHECKS
- All fasteners torqued or thread-locked as specified
- Bearings spin smoothly, no rough spots, no side play
- Chain/belt correctly tensioned and aligned
- No wires routed through pinch points or near moving parts
- Robot matches the Onshape assembly — no missing parts, nothing assembled backwards
- All retained hardware (cotter pins, snap rings, shaft collars) are in place and secured

DOCUMENTATION
After building, photograph the completed subsystem and note any deviations from the design (tolerance issues, hardware substitutions, field fixes). Update the Onshape model if a change was made during assembly so the CAD reflects the as-built robot.
