ELECTRICAL WIRING — GENERAL FAMILIARITY
Mentor Knowledge Guide — FIRST Robotics Team

LEVEL — GENERAL FAMILIARITY.
This file is for the AI to check whether a student could safely help wire
the robot. The goal is *general* understanding — not the exact wire gauge
or breaker amperage for every case. Quiz the student on safety, the big
picture of how power moves through the robot, the why behind choices, and
what could go wrong. Do NOT quiz on exact gauge-vs-amperage tables, exact
breaker ratings, or specific connector part numbers.

SAFETY FIRST
- Unplug the battery before working on the robot's electrical system. The
  main breaker is also off-switch — pop it if you're unsure.
- Don't wear loose jewelry or watches around the battery terminals; a
  short across them releases a lot of energy very fast.
- Wear safety glasses when cutting, stripping, or crimping. Tiny pieces
  of insulation and stray wire strands fly.
- If a wire is hot to the touch, or you smell something burning, stop
  and find a mentor.

THE BIG PICTURE
A robot's electrical system is a power tree:

  Battery → main breaker → power distribution board → individual
  breakers → motor controllers, RoboRIO (the brain), radio (wireless
  comms), and smaller branches for lights, sensors, etc.

The student should be able to trace power from the battery to a motor
and back. Specific board names and breaker amps do not matter.

CHOOSING WIRE SIZE (GENERAL IDEA, NOT A TABLE)
- THICKER wire is for HIGHER current. The bigger the current draw, the
  more copper you need to carry it without heating up or losing voltage.
- THINNER wire is fine for signals and low-current devices.
- A good general habit: when you don't know, ask a mentor or follow the
  team's existing convention rather than guess. Wires that are too thin
  for the current can heat up and melt insulation.

The student should understand the *principle* (thicker for higher
current). They should NOT be quizzed on specific gauges for specific
breaker sizes — that's a chart-lookup, not understanding.

WHEN TO CUT WIRES, AND HOW MUCH
- Measure the route first; don't free-cut and hope it reaches.
- Leave a little slack for movement, vibration, and future repairs —
  but not so much that a loop of wire can snag on something moving.
- After cutting, strip only the amount of insulation the connector
  needs. Bare copper sticking out past a connector is a short waiting
  to happen.

CRIMPING AND CONNECTIONS
- Most FRC connections are crimped, not soldered. A good crimp is
  mechanically strong and electrically solid. Soldering battery leads
  or motor leads is generally avoided.
- Tug-test every crimp before installing it. A connector that pulls off
  with a gentle tug will fail on the robot.
- Use the matching wire gauge for the connector — too thin and the
  crimp won't hold; too thick and it won't seat.

BREAKERS — THE CONCEPT
- A breaker is an automatic switch that trips when current goes above
  its rating, protecting wires and components downstream.
- The main breaker is large and protects the whole robot.
- Smaller breakers protect each branch (each motor, each subsystem).
- The student should know that the breaker rating must match the wire
  size — a breaker bigger than the wire can carry defeats the purpose.

CAN BUS (KEEP IT SIMPLE)
- Modern motor controllers and many sensors talk on a single shared
  pair of wires called the CAN bus.
- Each device on the bus has a unique ID. Two devices with the same ID
  is one of the most common bugs.
- The bus must be terminated correctly at both ends — usually the
  RoboRIO at one end and the power distribution board at the other.

COMMON MISTAKES TO RECOGNIZE
- Wire too thin for the current → heats up, smells bad, can melt.
- Loose connection → intermittent power, motor jitters, weird bugs.
- Two devices share a CAN ID → one or both don't show up in code.
- Forgot to put insulation back on a splice → potential short.
- Cable management bad → wires can get pinched by moving subsystems.

The student should be able to spot these in stories ("a motor was
working, then suddenly stopped — what would you check?") without
needing to recite procedures.

WHAT THE AI SHOULD AVOID ASKING
- Exact wire gauges paired with exact breaker amperages.
- Specific connector model names (Anderson, Wago, etc.).
- Specific PDH/PDP product names or pin layouts.
- Exact CAN bus termination resistance values.
- Anything that would require a chart lookup or memorization.

Ask about safety, the path of power, the *idea* behind choosing wire
size, the importance of solid connections, and how a student would
notice and respond to a problem. That's a comfortable general level.
