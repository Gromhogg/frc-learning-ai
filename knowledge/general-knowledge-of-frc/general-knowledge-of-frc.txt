GENERAL KNOWLEDGE OF FRC (FIRST ROBOTICS COMPETITION)
======================================================

LEVEL — GENERAL FAMILIARITY.
This file gives the AI mentor enough context to check whether a student has
a comfortable, practical understanding of what FRC is and how it works. It
is NOT meant to be quizzed on memorized details, exact numbers, or specific
part names. The student should be able to talk confidently about *concepts*,
not recite specs.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. WHAT FRC IS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- FRC stands for FIRST Robotics Competition — the high-school program in
  the FIRST family. FIRST = "For Inspiration and Recognition of Science
  and Technology."
- Teams of high schoolers, with mentor support, design and build a robot
  in a few weeks each year to compete in a new game revealed at Kickoff.
- Teams are identified by a permanent team number (e.g. "Team 972").
- The season ends at the FIRST Championship.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2. SEASON STRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- KICKOFF (early January): the new game is released. Teams start
  strategizing and prototyping right away.
- BUILD SEASON: January through February. Strategy, prototyping, CAD,
  fabrication, wiring, programming, and driver practice all happen in
  parallel.
- COMPETITION SEASON: March and April — District or Regional events.
- CHAMPIONSHIP: late April — the season's culminating event.
- OFF-SEASON: training, recruitment, outreach, informal off-season events.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
3. GAME STRUCTURE (EVERGREEN CONCEPTS)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A match is short — about two and a half minutes — split into three parts:

- AUTONOMOUS: the first stretch, no driver input. The robot runs
  pre-programmed routines.
- TELEOP: most of the match. Drivers control the robot from a driver
  station.
- ENDGAME: the final stretch. Usually has a bonus mechanic (climbing,
  parking, balancing) that changes by year.

Matches are 3-vs-3: a RED alliance plays a BLUE alliance.

Scoring changes every year, so the AI should ask about the *concepts*
(auto, teleop, endgame, alliances) not this season's specific points.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
4. EVENT STRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- QUALIFICATION MATCHES: each team plays a series of matches with random
  alliance partners. Results determine seeding.
- ALLIANCE SELECTION: the top-seeded teams pick partners to form alliances
  for the playoffs.
- PLAYOFFS: a bracket of alliances plays for the event title.
- DISTRICT vs REGIONAL: some regions play multiple smaller "District"
  events and advance on points; others play one larger "Regional" event
  with winners advancing.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
5. THE ROBOT — GENERAL RULES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- WEIGHT and SIZE LIMITS: every robot has to fit within a weight and
  size cap set by the game manual. Exact numbers change by year — the
  concept is that there IS a cap.
- BUMPERS: required. Wrap the perimeter of the robot, colored RED or
  BLUE depending on the alliance, with the team number visible.
- INSPECTION: every robot is checked before competing — weight,
  dimensions, bumpers, wiring, breakers, required safety lights.
- BATTERY: one 12-volt battery, swapped out between matches.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
6. ELECTRICAL SYSTEM (BIG PICTURE)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The electrical system is conceptually simple:

  Battery → Main breaker → Power distribution → Motor controllers,
  RoboRIO (the robot's computer), radio (for wireless comms), and
  smaller breakers for everything else.

What every student should be familiar with:
- ONE battery, ONE main breaker between it and everything else. The
  main breaker is the emergency "off switch."
- Power gets distributed through a central board, each branch on its
  own smaller breaker.
- The ROBORIO is the brain — runs the code, tells motor controllers
  what to do.
- The RADIO connects the robot to the driver station wirelessly.
- The CAN BUS is the wiring system most motor controllers talk on.
- The ROBOT SIGNAL LIGHT blinks when the robot is enabled.

Do NOT quiz on specific breaker amperages, wire gauges, model numbers,
or specs. The student should be able to explain the *flow* of power and
the role of each main component.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
7. MOTORS AND MOTOR CONTROLLERS (GENERAL)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- Robots use ELECTRIC MOTORS to drive wheels, intakes, shooters, arms,
  elevators, and climbers.
- Modern FRC uses BRUSHLESS motors almost exclusively — more efficient
  and more precise than older brushed motors.
- A MOTOR CONTROLLER takes commands from the RoboRIO and turns them
  into actual motor power. Sometimes it's built into the motor.

The student should know the concept (motor + controller, commanded by
code). They should NOT need to recite specific motor model names or
torque numbers.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
8. PNEUMATICS (OPTIONAL)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- Pneumatics use compressed air to push and pull pistons (cylinders).
  OPTIONAL — many modern designs skip them.
- A pneumatic system has a compressor, a tank, a regulator that limits
  working pressure, gauges, a relief valve, a vent valve, and solenoids
  that control the cylinders.
- The concept: "compressed air goes through a regulator to a cylinder,
  controlled by a solenoid the robot turns on and off." Specific PSI
  values don't matter.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
9. DRIVETRAINS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Three common types — recognize and roughly describe each:

- TANK / WEST-COAST: wheels on each side, all driven. Predictable.
  Cannot move sideways.
- MECANUM: special wheels with diagonal rollers — can move sideways.
  Less popular at the top level.
- SWERVE: each wheel both drives and steers independently. Full
  motion in any direction. Dominant in modern FRC, but complex.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
10. ROBOT SUBSYSTEMS (TYPICAL ROLES)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Most FRC robots are built from a small set of recurring subsystems:

- INTAKE: collects game pieces.
- INDEXER / FEEDER: queues or stores pieces inside the robot.
- SHOOTER / LAUNCHER: throws pieces toward a goal.
- ARM / ELEVATOR: extends the scoring mechanism to a position or height.
- ENDGAME MECHANISM: usually a climber or winch.
- VISION SYSTEM: a camera (sometimes with a small computer attached)
  that helps the robot see field markers or game pieces.

The student should understand what each subsystem is *for*, not memorize
the parts list of any particular year's robot.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
11. SENSORS (BIG PICTURE)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Robots use sensors to know what's going on:

- ENCODERS measure how far / how fast something has turned.
- LIMIT SWITCHES detect end-of-travel ("I've reached the top").
- A GYRO / IMU measures orientation and rotation.
- BEAM-BREAK or distance sensors detect game pieces.
- CAMERAS for vision.

The student should know these exist and roughly what each does. Specific
sensor models are not the point.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
12. PROGRAMMING (GENERAL)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- WPILib is the official FRC software framework. Most teams use Java.
- The robot has DISTINCT MODES — Disabled, Autonomous, Teleop, Test.
- Modern teams use COMMAND-BASED programming: subsystems wrap hardware
  (Drivetrain, Intake, Shooter); commands describe behavior (drive
  forward, run intake, score); commands are bound to buttons.
- AUTONOMOUS routines usually follow pre-planned paths and trigger
  scoring at specific points.

The student should understand the modes and the idea of "subsystems and
commands." They do NOT need to know specific WPILib class names.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
13. CONTROLS AND DRIVER STATION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- The DRIVE TEAM is a small group (driver, operator, human player, coach,
  technician) that runs the robot at events.
- The DRIVER STATION is a laptop running the official FRC software that
  sends controller inputs to the robot.
- At competitions, robots connect through the venue's FIELD MANAGEMENT
  SYSTEM (FMS), which enables/disables them on the official match clock.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
14. AWARDS (BIG ONES TO KNOW)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- IMPACT AWARD (formerly Chairman's): the highest award in FRC. Goes to
  the team with the broadest impact on STEM. Winning at an event
  qualifies a team for the Championship.
- ENGINEERING INSPIRATION: balances outreach with engineering strength.
- DEAN'S LIST: per-event award for outstanding student leaders.
- WOODIE FLOWERS AWARD: outstanding adult mentor.
- ROOKIE AWARDS: for first-year teams.

Plus event winners, finalists, and a rotating set of judged awards.

The student should know that there are *robot* awards and *judged*
awards, and recognize Impact and the Dean's List by name.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
15. CORE VALUES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- GRACIOUS PROFESSIONALISM (Woodie Flowers): compete hard, help your
  opponents, share what you know.
- COOPERTITION: cooperation and competition together.

The student should be able to describe what these mean in their own
words.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
16. TEAM ROLES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Most teams organize into sub-teams that mirror engineering disciplines:

- MECHANICAL / FABRICATION — building the physical robot.
- ELECTRICAL — wiring, controllers, power.
- PROGRAMMING — the robot's code.
- CAD / DESIGN — modeling the robot.
- DRIVE TEAM — operating it at events.
- STRATEGY / SCOUTING — game plans and alliance picks.
- BUSINESS / OUTREACH — sponsorship, awards, social media.
- SAFETY CAPTAIN — required at events.

The student should know sub-teams exist and roughly what they do.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
17. SHOP SAFETY (BASICS)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- Safety glasses, closed-toed shoes, hair tied back, no loose sleeves
  near rotating machinery.
- Hearing protection near loud tools.
- Unplug the battery before working inside the robot's electrical system.
- Vent pneumatic systems to zero pressure before opening any fitting.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
18. COMMON-SENSE TROUBLESHOOTING
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The student should be able to talk through what they'd CHECK in common
failure situations — not recite exact procedures:

- Robot won't enable → check radio / driver station status / battery.
- A motor doesn't move → check power, breaker, CAN ID, code.
- Pneumatics won't actuate → check compressor and pressure.
- Drivetrain pulls to one side → check encoders or a stuck wheel.
- Brownout (robot temporarily loses power) → battery too discharged or
  current draw too high; swap battery.

The right answer is the troubleshooting *instinct* — not the exact order
of steps.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
19. ACRONYMS TO RECOGNIZE (not memorize)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- FRC, FIRST, WPILib, RoboRIO, PDH/PDP, RSL, FMS, DS, COTS, CAN, IMU, RP.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
20. WHAT THE AI SHOULD AVOID ASKING
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- Exact specs (motor torques, breaker amperages, wire gauges, PSI
  values, exact dimensions).
- Specific part numbers, model names, or brand-specific terminology.
- Year-specific scoring values, point totals, or field measurements.
- Detailed WPILib class names or G-code syntax.
- Memorized acronym expansions — recognition is fine, recital is not.
- Anything that feels like a memory test rather than a check on
  understanding.
